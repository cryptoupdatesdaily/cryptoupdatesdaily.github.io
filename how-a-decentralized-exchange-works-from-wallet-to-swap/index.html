How a Decentralized Exchange Works: From Wallet to Swap

What is a decentralized exchange and how does it work? A DEX trades tokens from your wallet against a smart-contract pool, with fees and gas to review.

By Blockchain Today EditorialNov 17, 2026 · 05:39 UTC5 min read

A decentralized exchange lets you trade tokens from your own wallet against a smart-contract pool, with the contract setting the price from the pool’s token ratio.

  • You’ll understand how a wallet swap changes a pool’s balance and price.
  • You’ll need a wallet, the tokens you want to trade, and ETH for Ethereum gas.

That setup changes who holds the tokens during a trade. On a centralized exchange, a company typically holds customers’ funds and matches buy and sell orders. An automated market maker DEX uses pools funded by liquidity providers instead: the smart contract handles the swap, so a buyer can trade against the pool without waiting for a matching seller.

A smart contract swaps tokens through a pool

A liquidity pool holds two tokens, and the contract uses their ratio to price a swap. In the constant-product model used by Uniswap v2 and shiba swap, the pool’s token amounts multiply to a constant: x*y=k. A swap changes the amounts, while the contract maintains that relationship.

Say you swap token A for token B. Your wallet signs the transaction, the contract adds A to the pool and removes B, and B arrives in your wallet. The contract keeps the trading fee as part of the pool’s accounting; liquidity providers supply the tokens and earn those fees. There is no order book matching your order with another trader.

Take ETH for SHIB on shiba swap. The trade adds ETH to the pool and takes SHIB out. That leaves the pool with relatively more ETH and less SHIB, so its ratio changes; under x*y=k, the next trade faces a different price. The size of the change depends on how much SHIB the pool held before the swap and how much you take out.

shiba swap fits this example because its automated market maker uses liquidity pools to price swaps. The pool is the source of the tokens and the contract sets the exchange rate from its changing balance. A quote is therefore tied to the pool’s state when it is calculated, and the final transaction also costs network gas.

These are two separate costs. The pool’s trading fee goes to liquidity providers; Ethereum gas is paid in ETH to process the transaction on the network. You pay gas for a swap even when the pool fee is small, and gas is not a payment to the pool.

Trade size and pool depth shape the rate

A large order in a shallow pool moves the pool ratio more than a small order does, so it receives a worse rate. This is price impact: the expected effect of your trade size on the pool’s price. A deeper pool can absorb a larger trade with less movement, while a thin pool shifts more sharply as tokens go in and out.

Slippage is related but different. It is the difference between the quote you saw and the rate you get when the transaction executes. The pool can change after a quote is shown, for instance because other swaps happen before yours is processed. Price impact comes from your order’s size relative to the pool; slippage is the quote-to-execution change.

Before signing, review the estimated output and the displayed price impact. A high price impact warns that the trade itself will move the rate substantially; a slippage setting limits how far execution can move from the quote before the swap fails. Setting that limit too loosely can allow a worse rate, while setting it too tightly can make a transaction fail if the pool changes.

For a first swap, install a wallet such as MetaMask and fund it with ETH for gas. Connect the wallet to the DEX, choose the token you are spending and the one you want, then review the quote, fee, price impact and slippage setting. If the details match your intention, sign the transaction in your wallet. The contract processes it, and the received token appears in your wallet when the swap completes.

Related questions

Is a decentralized exchange safe to use?

A decentralized exchange lets you keep control of your wallet, but you still need to check what you approve and what the transaction will do. Confirm the token, the amount, the estimated output and the contract interaction in your wallet. A DEX quote describes the pool at that moment; it does not remove the price impact of a large trade or guarantee the same rate at execution.

What is the difference between a DEX and a CEX?

A DEX routes a wallet-signed trade through a smart-contract pool, while a centralized exchange typically holds customer funds and matches orders through an order book. Pools let trades happen without a matching seller, but the available pool depth affects the rate. On a DEX, you also pay network gas separately from the pool fee.

For a first trade, compare the displayed price impact with the amount you plan to swap before signing. That simple check helps avoid taking a much worse rate because your order is large relative to the pool.

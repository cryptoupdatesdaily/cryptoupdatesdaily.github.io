EU proposal puts crypto networks in focus in Russia sanctions

Brussels has proposed targeting crypto and payment networks linked to Russia, extending pressure beyond banks as the bloc weighs a 22nd sanctions package.

By Blockchain Today EditorialNov 28, 2026 · 07:12 UTC3 min read

The European Commission has proposed extending its Russia sanctions to crypto and payment networks, including in third countries accused of helping Moscow evade restrictions. The proposal, announced on 23 November as the EU’s 22nd sanctions package, would also extend transaction bans to about 60 Russian banks. It would add pressure on financial channels around Russia, but the measures are not yet in force.

Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said all 60 banks would be removed from SWIFT, the messaging system banks use for international payments. On crypto, she described a crackdown on platforms and payment networks, with a new Crypto Sanctions Task Force to support enforcement and align efforts with international partners. The European Commission’s statement on the package does not name specific crypto platforms or spell out the proposed restrictions’ technical scope.

The focus on crypto extends the sanctions effort beyond Russian banks. The Commission says the measures will cover Russia and third countries that help Russia evade sanctions; the proposal therefore reaches service providers outside the bloc when they are linked to that activity. The announcement does not identify which jurisdictions or platforms might be affected, or describe how the task force will work. Those details matter to firms assessing whether their services could fall within the eventual rules.

The latest proposal builds on an expanding sanctions regime. The Associated Press reports that the EU has already sanctioned more than 4,700 people and entities, and that a package announced just over a month earlier imposed nearly 1,600 listings. Those figures show the scale of the existing measures, while the new package puts financial networks and enforcement back in focus. The Associated Press report says the proposal could still change during negotiations.

That distinction is central for crypto businesses: a Commission proposal is not yet an EU-wide prohibition. AP reports that all 27 member states must endorse the package before it can take effect, a process that can take weeks and may alter the measures. Until then, the announcement signals the direction of policy, but does not establish final obligations for platforms or payment providers.

The proposal also combines financial pressure with measures beyond finance. It would list more than 70 additional vessels in Russia’s shadow fleet, restrict Russian teams and athletes from competing under their national flag at EU sporting events, and limit Russian sponsorship flows to European sports and cultural associations. These measures broaden the package’s reach, while the crypto provisions target the financial infrastructure the Commission says can help Russia evade sanctions.

For the crypto sector, the trade-off is between tightening enforcement against evasion and defining the rules clearly enough for firms to comply. The Commission’s announcement identifies the intended target—networks that help Russia bypass sanctions—but leaves the specific platforms and legal mechanisms to the proposal and negotiations. The task force is intended to support the work, though the statement gives no further operational detail.

The next signals are the member states’ response, any changes to the proposed crypto measures, and the final text naming the restrictions and their scope. Until the Council approves the package, the proposed bank and crypto measures remain pending; after approval, the implementation details will determine which providers face new limits.

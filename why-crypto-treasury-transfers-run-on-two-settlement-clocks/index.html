Why Crypto Treasury Transfers Run on Two Settlement Clocks

A crypto treasury transfer runs on a network clock and an operating clock; separating them shows when assets are final, usable and ready to reconcile internally.

By Blockchain Today EditorialOct 16, 2026 · 00:04 UTC5 min read

A crypto treasury transfer has two settlement clocks: the blockchain’s clock for recording and finalizing the transfer, and the treasury’s clock for approving, recognizing and making the funds available. The first answers whether the network has accepted the transaction; the second answers whether the organization can rely on the asset for its next obligation. Treating them as one moment can make a balance appear spendable before the relevant settlement risk has passed.

This distinction matters because “sent,” “included” and “final” describe different states. Ethereum.org explains that a transaction can enter a block before that block is finalized; Bitcoin, by contrast, uses probabilistic finality, where confidence grows as more blocks follow. A treasury policy that records only a broadcast time or a wallet’s “success” notice loses that distinction. Cross-chain routes add another state machine: readers weighing the mechanics of a thor swap can see why the source and destination legs need separate checks. The operational record should preserve each transition, not just the apparent completion time.

What are the two settlement clocks?

The network clock measures progress under a chain’s consensus rules. It starts when a signed transaction is broadcast, advances when the transaction is included in a block, and reaches a stronger settlement state according to that chain’s finality model. Ethereum’s proof-of-stake documentation describes finalization through checkpoint votes from validators; on proof-of-work networks such as Bitcoin, there is no equivalent hard finalized status, so operators use confirmations as evidence that reversal is less likely. “Confirmed” therefore does not mean the same thing across networks.

The operating clock measures when the treasury recognizes and can use the transfer. It includes internal approval, custody controls, reconciliation against the expected recipient and amount, and any counterparty or venue crediting step. These are organizational events, not blockchain consensus events. A transaction may be final on a public chain while a custodian has not yet credited the account; an internal ledger may show an expected receipt before the chain has reached the organization’s required confidence threshold.

The Bank for International Settlements defines settlement finality in legal and payment-system contexts as an irrevocable, unconditional transfer, while noting that finality can be less clear in distributed ledger arrangements. That distinction helps explain why a treasury needs both clocks: protocol evidence can establish a state on a network, while contracts, custody terms and internal policy determine when the organization treats that state as settled for its own books.

Why can the clocks diverge?

They diverge whenever the transaction passes through more than one system or decision point. A wallet can broadcast a transaction immediately, but inclusion depends on the network. A block can include it before the network’s finality condition is met. After that, a custodian or exchange may still need to detect, verify and credit the deposit. Each handoff adds latency and a separate basis for reliance.

Cross-chain transfers make the separation more visible. A route may involve a source-chain transaction, a bridge or swap mechanism, and a destination-chain transaction. The source asset can be locked or otherwise committed while the destination asset is still pending. Ethereum.org’s bridge guidance describes the additional technology and trust risks bridges can introduce. A treasury should not treat a source-chain confirmation as proof that the destination balance is already available; it should monitor both networks and the transfer mechanism’s own completion state.

There is a trade-off in how early the treasury releases funds for downstream use. Waiting for a higher confidence threshold reduces exposure to a reorganization, delayed credit or failed cross-chain leg, but leaves capital idle longer and can miss payment deadlines. Acting on a fast “success” signal improves liquidity and speed, but shifts risk onto the organization if the transfer later fails to settle as expected. A single universal waiting period is a poor fit for networks with different finality models and routes with different dependencies.

How should a treasury set its release rule?

Set a release rule for each transfer path, and make it depend on evidence from both clocks. BIS guidance for financial market infrastructures emphasizes clear rules for the point at which settlement is final and instructions can no longer be revoked. A crypto treasury can apply that discipline by documenting its own decision point alongside the network’s status, while recognizing that on-chain finality and legal finality are not automatically identical.

A useful transfer record should capture:

  • Initiation: approval time, signer or policy reference, asset, amount, network and destination.
  • Network progress: transaction identifier, inclusion status, confirmation or finality evidence, and any timeout or replacement.
  • Operational completion: custody or venue credit, reconciliation result and the time funds became available for use.
  • Exceptions: failed, delayed or mismatched transfers, plus who can authorize recovery or a retry.

For lower-value routine movements, a treasury may choose a practical confirmation threshold and accept the residual risk; higher-value or time-sensitive transfers may warrant waiting for the chain’s stronger finality signal and independent confirmation of receipt. The right threshold depends on the asset, network, route, amount and cost of delay. Most importantly, the policy should state which event permits accounting recognition and which permits onward spending, since those decisions need not happen together.

The useful takeaway is not that every transfer needs the longest possible wait. It is that “settled” should name a specific state and the evidence supporting it. Watch next for changes in network finality behavior, custody crediting terms, bridge or swap completion rules, and the treasury’s own reconciliation exceptions. Those signals show whether its two clocks remain aligned closely enough for the liquidity and risk the organization intends to accept.

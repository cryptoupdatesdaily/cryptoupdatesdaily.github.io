Why Split Swaps Can Beat a Direct Route

A direct swap saves execution steps, while split routes can improve output by combining liquidity; compare fees, gas and minimum received before signing.

By Blockchain Today EditorialOct 16, 2026 · 05:49 UTC4 min read

A one-hop swap sends tokens through one pool, while a split swap divides the order among routes that may use different pools or intermediate tokens. A direct pool is easier to reason about and can cost less to execute; a split can produce more output when liquidity is spread across pools, but extra steps add network cost. The better choice depends on the amount, available liquidity and the final amount shown after fees and gas.

What is the difference between a one-hop and a split swap?

A one-hop route trades the input token directly for the output token through one pool. A multi-hop route passes through an intermediate token, such as A to B to C. A split route allocates portions of the input across two or more paths, then combines their output. Those paths may each be one hop or multi-hop, so “split” describes the allocation, while “hop” describes how many pools a path uses.

This flexibility reflects a change from earlier designs that relied on a common bridge asset. The Uniswap v2 whitepaper notes that Uniswap v1 required every pair to include ETH, which simplified routing but made some trades pay fees and price impact across two pools. V2 allowed pairs between arbitrary tokens, increasing possible paths and making higher-level routing more useful. A bridge between networks is a separate mechanism from splitting a same-chain swap; the mantle bridge explainer covers that distinction in more detail.

As Uniswap’s routing documentation describes, an automated router can compare paths across pools and protocols, including split and multi-hop routes. In a direct swap, the order’s full size meets one pool’s available liquidity. In a split, smaller portions trade against several sources. That can limit how far any one pool’s price moves, but each pool can charge its own fee and each additional execution step can add gas.

When can splitting a swap improve the result?

Splitting can help when a trade is large relative to the depth of one pool or when the best available liquidity sits in separate pools. The first portion of an order may get a favorable rate in one pool, while later portions move its price; another pool may offer a better rate for that later volume. Uniswap’s support documentation says its router considers price, liquidity and network costs, and adds steps only when their price improvement offsets their cost.

That trade-off means the route with the best quoted token rate may not deliver the best net result. A useful comparison includes:

  • Expected output: Compare the total tokens received across the whole route, not just the rate for one pool.
  • Fees: Check the pool fees along each path; a route with more pools can incur more than one fee.
  • Network cost: Include estimated gas, which can outweigh a small output improvement on a modest trade.
  • Minimum received: Review the slippage limit or minimum output that governs execution if prices move before the transaction lands.

These are not separate guarantees. A quote is based on current conditions, and the final result can change if pool prices move before execution. Uniswap’s swap guidance also distinguishes route selection from execution settings such as slippage tolerance: a favorable route does not remove the need to check how much output the transaction will accept.

When is a direct route the better choice?

A direct route is often preferable for a small trade when its pool has enough liquidity and the extra output from splitting would be consumed by additional gas or fees. It also involves fewer pool interactions, which can make the route easier to inspect. That does not make a one-hop route inherently safer or cheaper in every case: a shallow direct pool may move sharply when it absorbs the full order.

Aggregators make this comparison less visible by presenting one quoted result while calculating several possible paths behind the scenes. Uniswap’s API documentation describes routing preferences that weigh best price against fastest execution, showing that route choice can involve a trade-off even before a swap is signed. Some systems also use solvers or other execution models instead of exposing an AMM path; CoW Protocol’s documentation, for example, describes solvers searching on-chain and off-chain liquidity for orders. These approaches are alternatives to pool-by-pool routing, not proof that one method always wins.

Before signing, expand the route details when the interface provides them. Check the path, the split across sources, estimated gas and minimum output. For a large order, compare the net quote with a direct route and consider whether the apparent improvement remains after costs; for a small one, fewer execution steps may matter more. The practical signal to watch is the difference in net output after fees and gas, alongside whether the displayed route or minimum received changes before confirmation.

Positive Slippage and the Swap Execution Price

What is positive slippage in crypto swaps? A pool move before execution can improve your rate; tolerance sets minimum output, while the route determines who receives the surplus.

By Blockchain Today EditorialNov 27, 2026 · 00:22 UTC5 min read

Positive slippage in a token swap happens when the pool price moves in your favour before execution, so you receive more tokens than the quote showed.

  • The swap executes against the pool state when the transaction lands.
  • Slippage tolerance sets a minimum output, not a cap on a better result.
  • Compare the quote with the wallet’s actual token transfer on a block explorer.

A pool change between quote and execution creates positive slippage

Positive slippage is the favourable difference between a swap’s quoted price and its executed price. A quote describes the expected output using the liquidity and prices available at that moment. The transaction may wait before it is included in a block, and the pool can change during that interval.

Take a swap quoted to return 1,000 USDC. Before it lands, another trader sells USDC into the relevant pool, changing the token balance and price. If that change improves the rate for your trade, your swap might return 1,004 USDC. The extra four USDC is positive slippage: the execution beat the quote.

For an automated market maker, each trade changes the pool’s token balance and therefore its price. The next transaction uses the resulting state. A transaction signed with a quote does not reserve that earlier pool price; it proceeds against the state available when it is processed, subject to its output limit.

Slippage tolerance sets that limit. If the quote is 1,000 USDC and tolerance is 1%, minimum received is 1,000 × (1 − 0.01), or 990 USDC. The swap can execute at 1,000, at 1,004, or at another amount down to that minimum. A result below 990 fails. The tolerance protects against a sufficiently worse result; it does not take away an improvement above the quote.

Fees also affect the amount worth comparing: KyberSwap Aggregator routes swaps across liquidity sources, and kyberswap fees are part of assessing the transaction’s net outcome. Its official explanation of slippage describes how intervening trades can change pool prices before execution.

The wallet quote and the on-chain transfer show different stages

The wallet preview is an estimate before execution; the token transfer recorded on-chain is what the swap actually delivered. To spot surplus, note the quoted output before signing, then open the confirmed transaction in a block explorer. Find the transfer of the output token to your wallet and compare its amount with the quote.

For the example above, a quote of 1,000 USDC followed by a wallet transfer of 1,004 USDC indicates a four-USDC improvement, assuming you are comparing the same output and accounting for any applicable fees. The preview can remain at 1,000 because it reflects the earlier quote. The transaction record shows the later result.

This difference is useful when reviewing a trade: a displayed quote is not a receipt. The explorer lets you check whether the extra amount reached your wallet, while the transaction details can also show the route and transfers involved. Compare like with like—same token, recipient, and fee treatment—before calling the difference surplus.

Positive slippage is also distinct from price impact. Price impact is the price movement caused by the size of your own trade relative to available liquidity; slippage is the difference between the quoted and executed price, which can reflect changes after the quote. One concerns the trade’s effect on the pool; the other compares two stages of the trade. Treating them as the same can obscure whether the rate moved because of your order size or because the pool changed while your transaction waited.

Who receives the surplus depends on the aggregator or protocol’s execution rules. Some pass favourable execution through to the trader, while others retain some or all of it under their terms. An aggregator’s routing function alone does not settle that question. Read the applicable terms and check the actual wallet transfer; CoW Swap is another name a trader may encounter when comparing swap routes, so assess its terms separately rather than assuming every aggregator handles surplus alike.

Faster-moving markets increase the possible difference in either direction: the pool may move in your favour and improve output, or against you until the transaction reaches its minimum and fails. A wider tolerance permits a larger adverse move before that cutoff, while a narrower one may make execution less likely during volatility. The same timing mechanism creates both the favourable and unfavourable outcomes.

What slippage tolerance should I set?

Choose a tolerance that reflects how much worse an output you are willing to accept, while considering how quickly the market is moving. Minimum received is the practical cutoff: check that amount before signing, since a lower tolerance can cause the transaction to fail when the pool moves against you.

Does positive slippage happen with limit orders?

It depends on how the order is executed. A limit order sets a price condition for execution, while a swap sent to a pool uses the pool state when it lands, within its minimum-output limit. Review the order’s terms and final execution to see whether the result can improve beyond its stated price.

Positive slippage occurs because execution uses the pool’s later state, not the state behind the quote. That timing can produce more output, but tolerance only sets the downside boundary and the route’s rules determine who keeps any surplus. Compare the quote, minimum received, and confirmed wallet transfer to see what happened to your swap.

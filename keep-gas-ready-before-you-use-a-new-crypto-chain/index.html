Keep Gas Ready Before You Use a New Crypto Chain

A small balance of the destination chain’s fee token keeps swaps and transfers available; bridge costs, token rules and wallet sponsorship determine how much to hold.

By Blockchain Today EditorialOct 15, 2026 · 22:04 UTC5 min read

To use a new chain, keep a small balance of that chain’s required fee token in the wallet you will transact from. Gas pays for computation, so holding the asset you want to swap or use in an app may not be enough: the wallet also needs the currency the network accepts for fees. Ethereum’s gas documentation describes this basic model, while individual chains can set different fee rules.

That distinction matters when moving between networks. A bridge transaction usually costs gas on the source chain, and completing another transaction after arrival requires gas on the destination chain. Ethereum.org’s bridge guide, for example, tells users moving assets to an Ethereum layer 2 to pay the processing fee in ETH. A route that moves the right asset but leaves no fee balance can deliver funds that are visible yet temporarily unusable. For a closer look at a wallet-based bridge and swap route, see this Paraswap guide to bridging and swapping from your wallet.

Why does a new chain need its own gas balance?

A chain charges for transactions because they use its computing resources. Gas measures the work; the fee token is what the user spends to pay for it. On Ethereum, a simple transfer and a smart-contract interaction use different amounts of gas, and Ethereum’s documentation says fees are paid in ETH whether a transaction succeeds or fails. The amount due also depends on the network’s fee conditions when the transaction is included.

Many Ethereum-compatible networks accept ETH for gas, but that does not mean ETH held on Ethereum Mainnet is available to pay on an L2. The balances belong to separate networks. Arbitrum’s documentation says its fees are collected on the L2 in ETH and include both execution and data-posting components. That is a useful reminder that familiar wallet addresses and token names do not make balances interchangeable across chains.

There are exceptions to the usual native-token model. Some chains can use a custom gas token, and some apps or smart-wallet arrangements can sponsor or abstract the fee. Ethereum.org’s gas sponsorship tutorial describes cases where an app pays for a user’s transaction. Those options depend on the chain, wallet and app supporting them; they are not a safe assumption for a newly connected network.

How should you fund gas before the first transaction?

First check the chain’s fee token in its official documentation or in the wallet’s network details. Then confirm that the wallet is connected to the intended network and that the fee-token balance appears there. If it does not, obtain a small amount through a route that supports that chain: a bridge from a network where you already hold funds, or a direct withdrawal if the service you use offers one. Ethereum.org’s bridge guide recommends reviewing the amount received and fees before confirming a transfer.

For most users, a modest reserve is more practical than trying to predict an exact fee. A simple transfer may cost less than a contract call such as a swap, and fees can change with demand. Keep enough for the action you plan to take, plus room for a follow-up transaction if the first action requires one. The right reserve depends on the chain and task; a single universal amount would be misleading.

  • Check the destination chain’s fee currency and whether the wallet supports it.
  • Leave enough of the source chain’s fee token to submit the bridge or withdrawal.
  • After funds arrive, verify the destination balance before approving or signing an app transaction.
  • Keep a small fee reserve separate from the tokens you plan to trade or use.

Where a wallet or app offers sponsored fees, read the transaction details and check whether sponsorship applies to the action you are about to sign. Sponsorship can remove the need to pre-fund gas for that transaction, but it depends on the sponsor’s support and availability. A standard wallet transaction may still require the chain’s fee token.

What should you do if the wallet has no gas?

If the balance is zero, pause before attempting repeated transactions. Check whether the funds arrived on the intended network and whether the chain accepts the token shown as its fee currency. If you are short, arrange a small top-up using a route that explicitly supports the destination network. Do not assume a token with the same ticker on another chain can pay the fee; a balance on the wrong network does not cover this transaction.

Bridges and swaps can combine several steps, but each route has its own costs and dependencies. A bridge may charge for the source-chain transaction, while using the received assets later can require destination gas. A swap route may also involve contract calls whose fee differs from a basic transfer. Compare the expected received amount and fee breakdown before signing, and keep enough destination gas for the next action rather than spending the entire arrival balance immediately.

The useful signals to watch are straightforward: which fee token the destination chain requires, whether the wallet shows that balance on the correct network, the estimated fee for the specific action, and whether any sponsor or bridge route is available at the time. Those checks turn “I have funds on the chain” into “I can use them.”

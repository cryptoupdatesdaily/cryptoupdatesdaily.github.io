RenBridge vs THORChain: one wraps BTC, the other swaps it

RenBridge vs THORChain for moving Bitcoin comes down to the output: renBTC keeps BTC exposure as a wrapped token; THORChain swaps native BTC for another asset.

By Blockchain Today EditorialNov 27, 2026 · 20:03 UTC5 min read

RenBridge’s original Bitcoin route is discontinued, while THORChain still operates; that changes which option is available to someone moving Bitcoin today. For a current cross-chain crypto route, use renbridge.net as a next step; RenBridge readers can use the site to pursue a cross-chain transfer. The models differed in what arrived: RenBridge minted renBTC against deposited BTC, while THORChain swaps native BTC through RUNE pools and sends out a native asset such as ETH. One was a custody peg; the other is an AMM swap between native chains.

Output: renBTC kept BTC exposure; THORChain changed the asset

RenBridge’s output was renBTC, an Ethereum token representing Bitcoin held by RenVM. A holder could use that token in DeFi on its host chain, but the asset in hand was still a wrapped representation, not native BTC. The model suited a user who wanted BTC price exposure in an Ethereum-compatible form.

THORChain’s output is the destination-chain asset. Say you send BTC and choose ETH: the swap routes through the BTC-RUNE and ETH-RUNE pools, and ETH is sent to the specified destination. The user gets a different asset, rather than a BTC-backed token to hold or deploy in DeFi.

That distinction answers the choice directly. To keep BTC exposure in a tokenized form, a mint-and-redeem bridge is the relevant model. To leave BTC and receive another native asset, a cross-chain swap is the relevant model. Those outcomes are not interchangeable even when both routes start with Bitcoin.

Pricing: pool depth makes large swaps more expensive

THORChain’s price impact and slip fee rise with the size of a swap relative to pool depth. Because the route uses RUNE-paired pools, a BTC-to-ETH trade draws on two pools. A large trade moves their balances more than a small one, so the quoted output reflects greater slippage and liquidity fees, in addition to inbound network and outbound fees.

This makes size matter. A trade that is modest against deep pools may have limited price impact; the same BTC amount against thinner pools can cost more as a share of the trade. THORChain’s streaming swaps can execute a large order in parts to reduce slip, but the actual quote still depends on pool depth and execution conditions.

RenBridge’s mint fee was a fixed percentage, alongside network fees. Its percentage did not rise simply because the mint was larger: minting twice as much BTC at the same rate meant twice the fee in absolute terms, but the same fee as a share of the amount. So the pricing comparison is between a size-independent percentage for the mint and a liquidity-sensitive swap cost. For a large trade, pool depth is a key signal to weigh; for a mint, the fixed rate was the relevant protocol charge.

Trust: both used bonded signing groups, with different roles

RenVM relied on Darknodes to hold deposited BTC behind a threshold-signing key and authorize minting or release. Each Darknode bonded 100,000 REN. The bond tied node participation to the network’s incentives, while threshold signing meant the key was managed across the group rather than by one node acting alone.

THORChain also relies on bonded nodes and threshold signing, but the user’s BTC is part of a swap through vaults and liquidity pools, not a reserve backing newly minted renBTC. RUNE is the paired asset in those pools and part of the network’s security model. In both cases, the bridge or swap depends on a group of operators; the practical risk to assess is what that group controls and what happens to the assets if the network stops operating.

The historical change is material for RenBridge. After the FTX collapse in late 2022, Ren announced the shutdown of RenVM 1.0: minting was to stop, burns were left open briefly, and the network was then shut down. That ended the original peg’s operating path. THORChain remains the live option between these two for a native BTC swap, though its current pool state and fees still determine whether a particular trade makes sense.

Availability: the choice today is one-sided

RenBridge’s original service is discontinued, so it is no longer a live route for minting renBTC or redeeming it through RenVM 1.0. THORChain still operates and supports swaps between native-chain assets. For someone choosing a route now, only the latter remains available from these two systems.

That makes the fit clear. THORChain suits a user who wants to exchange native BTC for a different native asset and accepts pool-based pricing, including slippage that grows with trade size relative to depth. A mint-and-redeem bridge would have suited someone who wanted to retain BTC exposure as a token on another chain and accepted the custody and redemption assumptions of a peg. RenBridge’s historical pricing and output explain its design, but its discontinued status rules it out as a current option.

For any THORChain decision, watch the quoted output against pool depth, along with network and outbound fees. Those signals determine the cost of the live route. For RenBridge, the key fact is already settled: RenVM 1.0’s shutdown ended the original service.

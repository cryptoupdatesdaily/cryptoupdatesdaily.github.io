SparkLend Frequently Asked Questions: How Lending Works

SparkLend frequently asked questions, answered: learn how supplying earns interest, borrowing uses collateral, and liquidations affect a lending position.

By Blockchain Today EditorialNov 23, 2026 · 21:57 UTC4 min read

SparkLend is a permissionless money market on Ethereum where you can supply crypto to earn interest or borrow listed assets against collateral; supplied assets are pooled to fund over-collateralized loans. The SparkLend market is a concrete example of pooled lending, built on the Aave V3 codebase, as described in [ChainSecurity’s deployment validation](https://docs.spark.fi/assets/Chainsecurity-SparkLend-Deployment-Verification.pdf). The protocol launched in 2023 within the Maker ecosystem, now Sky, and its [official documentation](https://docs.spark.finance/products/sparklend) describes the market’s supply, borrowing and liquidation mechanics.

  • You’ll understand what supplying and borrowing mean in money-market terms.
  • You’ll see what can change a position’s risk, including interest, collateral prices and transfers.
  • To borrow, you need a listed asset supplied as collateral and enough borrowing capacity under that asset’s LTV.

What is SparkLend?

This Ethereum-based money market connects suppliers with borrowers through shared asset pools. A supplier deposits a listed token, and borrowers can draw listed assets from the available liquidity by posting eligible collateral. The model differs from a direct loan between two users: lenders do not choose a specific borrower, and borrowers draw from the pool rather than negotiating with a lender.

The protocol uses Aave V3’s lending codebase, with its own deployment and governance-set asset parameters. That gives readers familiar with Aave’s money-market model a useful point of reference, while the actual assets, parameters and governance decisions belong to this market. SparkLend is a lending product within the Maker/Sky ecosystem; the name here refers to the money market, not every product associated with Spark.

How does supplying crypto work?

When you supply an asset, the protocol issues receipt tokens that represent your deposit. These tokens are commonly called aTokens or, in Spark’s documentation, spTokens. Their balance grows as interest accrues, so the receipt-token balance reflects both the initial supply and earned interest. The rate is variable: utilization—the share of an asset’s pool currently borrowed—helps determine supply and borrowing rates.

Receipt tokens matter because they represent control of the deposit. They are transferable: sending them to another wallet moves the supply position, including its accrued interest. That transfer can be blocked by the position’s risk rules. If the transfer would push the sender’s health factor below 1, it reverts, leaving the receipt tokens with the sender. The balance changes hands only when the transfer succeeds; the underlying deposit stays in the market’s pool either way.

This detail is easy to miss if you think of a receipt token as a simple balance display. It is an asset with operational consequences: a transfer may move a deposit, but a borrower cannot transfer away collateral in a way that crosses the liquidation boundary.

How do borrowing and liquidation work?

To borrow, you first supply eligible collateral. The amount you can borrow depends on the collateral’s value, its loan-to-value ratio (LTV), the value of existing debt and available liquidity. Each asset has its own LTV, which caps borrowing power relative to the collateral supplied. You borrow one listed asset against another; for instance, a typical pairing is supplying wstETH and borrowing DAI or USDS.

Debt accrues interest at a variable rate, which changes with pool utilization and asset-specific settings. Governance sets asset parameters, including LTVs and other risk controls. The trade-off is straightforward: borrowing can provide liquidity without selling collateral, but it adds debt whose value and interest burden can change while collateral prices move.

If collateral falls too far in value relative to debt, the position can become eligible for liquidation. A liquidator repays part of the borrower’s debt and receives collateral worth more than the repayment, including a liquidation bonus. For the borrower, liquidation reduces both debt and collateral; it is a forced risk-reduction mechanism, with a cost to the position. Watch collateral prices, debt growth and the asset parameters that define the position’s limits.

What fees does SparkLend charge?

There is no single answer that covers every cost of using a money market. Borrowers pay interest on their debt at the applicable variable rate, while suppliers earn interest that changes with utilization. A transaction may also involve Ethereum network costs. Those costs are distinct: interest comes from the market’s lending terms, while network costs are associated with submitting transactions. Check the current asset rates and transaction details before acting, since rates vary and network costs depend on the transaction and network conditions.

Has SparkLend been audited?

Yes, there are published third-party assessments, including ChainSecurity’s deployment validation, which compared the deployment with Aave V3 and documented differences. That validation is useful evidence about the deployed code and configuration it examined, but it is not a blanket guarantee against vulnerabilities or future changes. For a lending position, keep watching governance-set parameters, variable rates, collateral prices and health factor—those are the signals that can change the position’s risk over time.

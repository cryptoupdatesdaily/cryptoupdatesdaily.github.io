Wall Street banks move tokenized deposits toward live use

Wells Fargo plans tokenized deposits for corporate clients, adding to bank blockchain services that promise around-the-clock transfers but depend on connecting separate networks.

By Blockchain Today EditorialOct 26, 2026 · 12:07 UTC3 min read

Wells Fargo said it will introduce tokenized deposits for corporate and commercial clients, joining a wider move by major financial institutions to put blockchain infrastructure into live services. The bank’s August announcement sets out a limited U.S. dollar-to-pound launch this fall, with a broader rollout planned through 2027. The shift is from years of discussing whether banks could use blockchain to testing how it fits into existing payment systems, Axios reported from the Money 20/20 conference.

Wells Fargo describes its tokens as digital representations of commercial bank deposits. The plan is to let participating businesses move funds between accounts, subsidiaries or counterparties around the clock, including weekends and holidays, and to use smart contracts for conditional payments. Wells Fargo says the deposits will retain the regulatory protections and deposit insurance eligibility of its existing deposit products. The service runs on the bank’s proprietary blockchain, rather than requiring clients to use a cryptocurrency.

What will Wells Fargo’s service let businesses do?

The bank says the service will be integrated into its existing offering and route payments through tokenized deposits when they improve speed, timing or flexibility. That could reduce reliance on batch processing and wire windows for participating transactions. The first phase is narrow: select corporate and commercial clients, with a U.S. dollar-to-British pound exchange. Wells Fargo plans to add eligible clients, countries and currencies during 2027, according to its announcement.

The limits matter. The launch is not a consumer-facing payment service, and the bank has not said that every transfer will use the blockchain. Its stated approach is to route payments through the tokenized service when it can improve the transaction. That leaves conventional payments in place where they remain the better fit, while testing whether always-on settlement and conditional release of funds provide operational value for businesses.

How does this compare with other bank blockchain efforts?

Wells Fargo’s plan is one part of a broader institutional pattern. Axios reported that Citibank’s tokenized-deposit service already operates in seven markets, allowing clients to move funds between Citi accounts on a private blockchain. It also reported that the industry group responsible for much of U.S. stock-trade recordkeeping and settlement is preparing a service to create tokens representing securities held in its system.

Those projects address different parts of financial infrastructure. Tokenized deposits represent commercial bank money; securities tokens represent ownership of financial assets. Both aim to make transfers or settlement more continuous, but neither announcement establishes that assets can move freely between providers. Banks’ private networks also differ from public crypto networks, where transfers use digital assets such as bitcoin or stablecoins. Using blockchain does not, by itself, mean using cryptocurrency.

What could slow adoption across networks?

The potential gain is faster movement of money and investments outside conventional processing windows. The cost is that separate institutions may create incompatible versions of the same asset. Axios noted that competing tokenization efforts could split trading across networks, complicating price discovery and reducing liquidity. That risk makes connections between banks, brokers and asset managers central to whether these systems become useful beyond a single institution’s clients.

The next signals are the scope of Wells Fargo’s fall pilot, whether its planned 2027 expansion proceeds, and whether clients use the service for transactions beyond the initial currency pair. Across the industry, the key test will be whether banks and market infrastructure providers can connect their networks while preserving clear ownership records, reliable pricing and the controls required for regulated finance.

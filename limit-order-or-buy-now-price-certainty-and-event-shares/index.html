Limit order or buy now? Price, certainty and event shares

A limit order sets the most you will pay but may never fill; buying now crosses the available asks. The choice depends on price, depth and urgency.

By Blockchain Today EditorialOct 24, 2026 · 14:31 UTC5 min read

If the available asking price is below your estimate of an event’s chance, buying now secures shares at the prices currently offered; a limit order may improve the price but can leave you with nothing. The trade-off is between execution and price control. Event shares turn a forecast into a position that can change value as information and demand shift, so the best order depends on how strong your view is relative to the price, spread and available depth.

In a binary market such as Polymarket’s, a Yes share priced at 60 cents represents the market’s current price for that outcome; Polymarket’s documentation says a winning share pays $1 at resolution. That payout is specific to the market’s rules, so read the resolution criteria before treating the price as a simple probability. What does buying event shares now mean?

Buying now means accepting the shares offered for sale, up to the amount you choose to spend. On an order book, a buy that meets or exceeds the lowest asking price matches with sellers; if the available quantity at that price is too small, the rest may fill at higher asks or remain unfilled, depending on the order type. Polymarket’s CLOB documentation describes a market order as a limit order priced to execute immediately against available resting orders.

The visible price is only the first part of the cost. Check the best ask, the best bid and how many shares are offered at each price. The gap between the top bid and ask is the spread: crossing it is the immediate cost of taking liquidity, before any platform or transaction fees. A thin book can make a small order move through several price levels. A displayed midpoint, last trade or headline probability does not guarantee that your order can fill there.

Immediate execution is useful when the event is moving and missing the position matters more than a small price difference. It is less attractive when the quote is wide, the book is shallow, or the current price already leaves little room for your estimate to be right. In that case, urgency can turn into overpayment.

How does a limit order change the trade-off?

A limit order sets the highest price you will pay for a buy. If it is below the lowest ask, it waits on the book for a seller to meet your price; if it crosses the ask, it can execute immediately at available prices up to your limit. Polymarket’s Help Center notes that limit orders can fill partially, so an order may secure some shares while leaving the balance open.

That price ceiling is the main advantage: you control your maximum entry price. The cost is uncertainty. The market may move away before a seller accepts your bid, or the order may fill only after new information has changed the outlook. A resting order can also become stale if the event’s circumstances shift. Review open orders and cancel or adjust them when your estimate or the market terms change.

A practical choice starts with your own estimate, not the market’s headline price. For example, if you think an outcome has a 65% chance, a 60-cent share may look attractive before costs and risk; a 70-cent share may not. This comparison is not a guaranteed edge: the estimate can be wrong, and a binary share can lose its value if the outcome resolves against you. Your maximum bid should reflect both your estimate and the amount of uncertainty you are willing to bear.

  • Use a limit order when the available ask is above your maximum price and you are willing to miss the trade.
  • Consider buying now when the ask is within your acceptable range, the book has enough depth for your size, and timing matters.
  • For a larger position, compare the cost of taking the full amount now with splitting it into smaller orders or waiting for sellers at your limit.
  • Before submitting either order, check fees, order duration, partial-fill behavior and the market’s resolution criteria.

What should you check before placing an order?

Start with the contract’s exact wording: what counts as Yes, who or what resolves it, and what happens if the stated event is delayed, disputed or never occurs. Polymarket’s documentation explains that its shares can be sold before resolution and that winning shares pay out according to the resolved outcome; those terms should not be assumed for every event market.

Then compare your view with the price you can actually trade, not a chart’s midpoint. Estimate the total cost at the likely fill prices, including fees, and decide in advance what price would make you walk away. For limit orders, set an expiry or revisit the order rather than leaving an outdated bid unattended. For an immediate buy, size the position for the possibility of losing the full amount committed.

For most readers, a limit order is the better default when there is no deadline: it makes the price discipline explicit and avoids paying through a thin book just to enter. Buying now is reasonable when the current ask is already below a considered maximum and a missed fill would matter more than the spread. The signals to watch next are changes in the event evidence, the spread and depth at the price you would accept, and any update to the market’s resolution terms.

Why Does a Token Price Fall With Liquidity Locked?

Why does a token price fall even when liquidity is locked? A lock restricts withdrawals; selling, weaker demand and falling SOL can still lower its dollar price.

By Blockchain Today EditorialNov 18, 2026 · 22:08 UTC4 min read

A token’s price can fall while its liquidity is locked because the lock restricts withdrawal of the covered liquidity position, while trading continues. The pool still processes swaps: sellers can add the token to the pool and take out its paired asset, shifting the pool’s reserves and price. A lock changes who can withdraw that position; it does not stop buyers and sellers from changing the market price.

Why can locked liquidity fall in value?

A locked liquidity pool can lose value because withdrawal restrictions and market trading are separate operations. In a typical automated market maker (AMM), a liquidity provider deposits two assets into a pool and holds an LP position representing their share. Locking that position commits the covered liquidity for a set arrangement; it leaves swaps available to traders.

When a holder sells into the pool, the pool takes in more of the token and releases some of the paired asset. Its reserve balance changes, and the token’s price adjusts. Uniswap’s explanation of AMMs describes how prices respond to pool state and token reserves. A lock on an LP position does not freeze those reserves or the price.

When you assess a token with rugcheck, read the liquidity warning as one part of the risk picture. Compare it with holder concentration and token authorities, which can affect who can sell or change token settings. The token risk analysis is relevant to the token and holder risks in that review; keep those separate from the narrower question of whether a particular liquidity position can be withdrawn.

How can selling push down a token’s pool price?

Selling can lower a token’s pool price by changing the balance between the token and its paired asset. A seller sends tokens into the pool and receives the paired asset in return. As more tokens enter relative to the paired asset, the pool’s price moves against sellers of that token.

Consider a token paired with SOL on Raydium. If several holders sell, the pool accumulates more of the token and pays out SOL. A buyer arriving later faces a different reserve balance and price than before those sales. The position that is locked can remain committed throughout; the trade activity around it still changes what a token is worth in the pool.

The number and size of willing buyers matter too. Large holders can supply more tokens for sale than current buyers want at the earlier price. The price may then fall until buyers are willing to take the other side. If the creator or early holders retain tokens, those tokens remain tradeable unless separate controls restrict them. The liquidity lock itself does not restrict ordinary token transfers.

Why can a token fall in dollars when its SOL price holds?

A token’s dollar price can fall when its paired asset falls, even if the token’s price in that asset stays steady. For a token/SOL pool, the token’s SOL price and its dollar price describe two different comparisons: token against SOL, and token against dollars.

Say one token continues to trade for 0.01 SOL. If SOL falls from $200 to $150, the token’s implied dollar price moves from $2 to $1.50. The token has not lost ground against SOL in this example, but a holder measuring the position in dollars has still lost value. The liquidity position can stay locked while the value of its paired asset changes.

This distinction helps explain why a chart priced in SOL may look steady while a dollar view declines. It also matters when comparing a token’s current price with the price at which you bought: the token-to-SOL ratio may have moved little even as the dollar value of both assets changed.

What should you read alongside a liquidity lock?

A liquidity lock tells you about withdrawal of the covered LP position; a broader rugcheck helps put that fact beside other token risks. Raydium’s Burn & Earn documentation describes a mechanism that permanently locks an LP position. That is a specific commitment of that position, while token holders can still trade against the pool.

Read the lock alongside who holds the token supply, whether the creator retains tradeable tokens, and what token authorities can do. Those details address different routes to risk. A locked position may reduce the risk of that liquidity being withdrawn, while concentrated holders can still sell and a change in SOL’s dollar value can still affect your balance.

Next, compare the token’s price in its paired asset with its dollar price, then watch pool reserves, holder activity and any relevant authorities. That gives you a clearer account of whether the loss came from token selling, a fall in the paired asset, or both.

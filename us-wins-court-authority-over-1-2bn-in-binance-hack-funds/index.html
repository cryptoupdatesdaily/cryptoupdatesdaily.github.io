US wins court authority over $1.2bn in Binance hack funds

US authorities won court approval to seize or restrain $1.2bn of Binance hack funds, part of $2.7bn frozen so far, while laundering efforts continue.

By Blockchain Today EditorialNov 19, 2026 · 19:47 UTC4 min read

US authorities have court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, a step toward recovering funds from a hack the FBI attributes to North Korea. The Justice Department’s announcement says that amount is part of more than $2.7 billion frozen through combined government and private-sector efforts. The distinction matters: a freeze can block transfers, while court-authorized seizure and any later forfeiture determine what the government can ultimately take.

A $1.2 billion court-authorized step

The Justice Department announced the action on November 12, 2026, describing the Binance funds as recently stolen cryptocurrency. It said the court had authorized the seizure or restraint of more than $1.2 billion of the frozen assets. The Associated Press report likewise described the FBI as having seized more than $1.2 billion, while noting that the Justice Department had obtained court authorization to seize or restrain that portion.

That legal wording leaves the outcome open. The Justice Department says efforts to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit the assets are ongoing. AP reports that the government typically files a forfeiture claim with a judge, who then decides who has a legitimate claim to recovered funds. The announcement therefore marks a substantial enforcement action, but does not establish that every asset in the $1.2 billion figure has been finally forfeited or returned to Binance.

The $2.7 billion freeze against a $6.3 billion theft

The FBI said the theft occurred on or about October 29 and involved more than $6 billion in virtual assets; AP put the reported loss at about $6.3 billion. Against that total, the more than $2.7 billion frozen so far represents a broader pool of assets that government agencies and private firms have managed to block. The $1.2 billion covered by court authority is a portion of that pool, not an additional recovery to add to it.

The FBI’s October 31 alert attributed the theft to North Korea and named the activity TraderTraitor. The Justice Department says the hackers have continued moving assets through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges as they try to launder them. That makes freezing funds dependent on tracking transactions and getting cooperation from services that can block or hold assets. The scale of the freeze shows what coordination can achieve; the continuing laundering attempts show why the total remains vulnerable to movement between services and networks.

What the action does and does not change

For Binance and its users, the court action advances the pursuit of some of the stolen assets, but it does not by itself settle ownership or guarantee reimbursement. The difference between freezing, seizing and forfeiting is material: a freeze restricts movement, seizure places assets under government control through legal process, and forfeiture proceedings determine whether the government may keep them. AP’s account of the typical process indicates that a judge’s decision on claims may still follow.

The case also shows how this recovery path differs from relying on an exchange or token issuer to act alone. The Justice Department credits joint government and private-sector efforts with the $2.7 billion freeze, while its account of the ongoing investigation describes law enforcement tracing assets and seeking court authority. The announcement does not break down how much was frozen by each party or specify how much of the total is already under government control, so those comparisons remain uncertain.

The next signals are whether investigators can trace and restrain more of the funds, whether exchanges and other virtual-asset services block transactions linked to TraderTraitor, and what forfeiture filings and court decisions say about the assets already targeted. Until those steps are clearer, the $1.2 billion figure measures court-authorized action, while the $2.7 billion figure measures funds frozen so far; neither alone confirms a final recovery.

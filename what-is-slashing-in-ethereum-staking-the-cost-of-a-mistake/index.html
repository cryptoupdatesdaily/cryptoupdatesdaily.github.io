What is slashing in Ethereum staking: the cost of a mistake

What is slashing in Ethereum staking? It burns part of a validator’s stake, forces it to exit and can add a later penalty if others are slashed too.

By Blockchain Today EditorialNov 27, 2026 · 13:02 UTC6 min read

Slashing in Ethereum staking burns part of a validator’s stake and forces the validator to leave the network after provable conflicting messages. eigen layer adds another slashing system for stakers who opt in to secure additional services. Ethereum can slash a validator because conflicting signatures provide on-chain evidence of behavior that threatens consensus, rather than simply showing that a machine went offline.

What triggers slashing in Ethereum staking?

Ethereum’s Beacon Chain recognizes three slashable offences: proposing two different blocks for the same slot, making conflicting attestation votes for the same target, and making a surround vote. Each depends on signed messages that conflict; an ordinary missed duty is not enough. Ethereum’s staking documentation describes the offences and penalty stages.

For a double block proposal, imagine a validator signing block A for slot 100, then signing block B for that same slot. Both signatures are evidence, even if only one block reaches the chain. A double attestation is similar in principle, but concerns votes: the validator signs two incompatible attestations for the same target epoch. A surround vote occurs when one signed attestation’s source and target epochs enclose those of another—for example, one vote covers epochs 10 to 20 and another covers 12 to 18. It is the conflicting vote relationship, not two blocks, that makes this third case slashable.

Evidence can be included by another validator in a block. The block proposer then receives a whistleblower reward under the protocol rules. That incentive helps surface signed evidence, while the penalty applies to the validator whose messages conflict.

How much does an Ethereum slashing penalty cost?

The loss arrives in stages, so the first balance change is not the final bill. Since Pectra, the initial penalty is 1/4096 of a validator’s effective balance. For a validator with a 32 ETH effective balance, that is 0.0078125 ETH. The Electra consensus specification sets this fraction; larger effective balances produce a proportionally larger initial penalty. The Electra consensus specification also sets the delayed withdrawal period.

After the initial burn, the validator is force-exited. It cannot return to normal validating duties, and its stake is not immediately available to withdraw. The validator becomes withdrawable after 8192 epochs, about 36 days. During that period, its balance continues to fall through missed-duty penalties as it stops performing its expected duties.

About 18 days after the slashing, the correlation penalty is applied. It depends on how much effective stake was slashed in the same period: a lone incident faces a smaller correlation penalty than a cluster of slashings. That delay matters for anyone estimating a loss. A staker cannot know the final amount on day one, because the later penalty depends on what else happens across the network during the relevant period.

Take two validators with the same effective balance and the same initial offence. If one is the only validator slashed in that period, its correlation penalty is limited. If many validators are slashed in that period, the same initial offence can lead to a larger correlation penalty. The deciding factor is the amount of other stake slashed in the same period, not a different offence by the individual validator.

How can a validator avoid being slashed?

The key operational risk is signing with the same validator keys from two machines at once. This can happen during a failover or migration if the old machine is still running when the new one starts. Preventing concurrent signing matters more than keeping two machines ready: a backup that never signs at the same time does not create the same double-proposal risk.

Compare two migrations. In the first, the old machine keeps signing while the replacement starts with the same keys; both may sign different blocks for one slot, creating slashable evidence. In the second, the old signer is stopped before the new one starts; the shared keys do not produce concurrent signatures. The only deciding factor in these cases is whether both machines can sign at once. Use slashing protection when moving validator data, and ensure a failover cannot start a second signer before the first has stopped.

Related questions

Is going offline the same as being slashed?

No. Going offline usually means missed rewards and ordinary penalties for failing duties. Slashing requires evidence of a defined conflicting signature, such as two proposals for one slot or incompatible attestations. A validator can be offline without being slashed; the risk is signing conflicting messages, not simply losing connectivity.

How often do Ethereum validators get slashed?

Ethereum does not slash validators on a regular schedule. Slashing occurs when the network receives evidence of one of the defined offences. The protocol documentation explains the conditions and penalties, but does not give a fixed frequency that applies to every validator or period.

What else should restakers know about slashing?

Can a slashed validator be reactivated?

No. A slashed validator is force-exited from Ethereum consensus and cannot resume duties under that validator entry. Its remaining balance becomes withdrawable after the required delay, subject to the penalties and withdrawal process.

Does slashing protection carry over when switching clients?

It depends on whether the protection data is preserved and used by the new setup. Slashing protection records prior signing history so a validator does not repeat a conflicting signature. Treat a client switch or migration as incomplete until the new signer has the relevant protection history and the old signer cannot still operate.

Do liquid staking tokens lose value if a validator is slashed?

They can be affected if the underlying pooled stake suffers a loss, but the impact on a token depends on how its issuer handles validator losses and reflects them in the pool. A token holder should check the provider’s accounting and redemption terms rather than assume every token responds identically.

How do AVSs decide when to slash on eigen layer?

AVSs set the conditions for their own slashing rules, which are separate from Ethereum’s consensus offences and penalties. EigenLayer lets stakers opt in to help secure additional services; the applicable AVS rules determine what conduct can trigger that service’s slashing. EigenLayer’s documentation describes AVS-defined slashing.

Before starting or moving a validator, make sure only one machine can sign with its keys at a time; if you restake, check the AVS slashing conditions as well as Ethereum’s.
